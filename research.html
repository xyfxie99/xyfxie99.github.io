Delayed Substitution and Price-Tier Dynamics in Household Food Consumption

with Bulat Gafarov and Jens Hilscher · Draft available on request

Within U.S. grocery categories, cheap products have seen greater price growth than expensive ones, a pattern known as cheapflation. Using the Nielsen Consumer Panel data for 2004–2024 and classifying products within each category into cheap and expensive tiers by their entry prices, we show that cheapflation is concentrated in downturns. It is strongest and most widespread during the Great Recession and close to zero in normal times. Low-income households, who devote a larger share of their grocery budget to these products, are the most exposed. We estimate the elasticity of substitution between low- and high-price tiers within product modules in a CES framework, using local projections to trace household responses over four quarters. Substitution is sluggish. Households shift little spending between tiers when relative prices change, and their response takes about a year to build up. Low-income households substitute more than high-income households, and their substitution keeps deepening over the year, while that of high-income households changes little. Using the long-run elasticity, we find that expensive tiers carry higher implied markups than cheap tiers. Observed price ratios between tiers, however, are unrelated to the markup ratios. Current relative tier prices do not reflect households' long-run substitution behavior, which suggests that they are not set optimally.

Health Information and Food Purchasing Responses: Evidence from a Type 2 Diabetes Diagnosis

with Kristin Kiesel

Summary

Combines the Nielsen Consumer Panel with PanelViews survey data and employs a staggered difference-in-differences estimator. Finds that a diabetes diagnosis triggers significant, persistent reductions in non-fresh produce purchases, with sharp heterogeneity by income.

Market Power and Food Price Pass-Through: Evidence from Restaurants and Grocery Retail

Summary

Links Datassential restaurant menu prices with Nielsen data to compare cost pass-through across sectors with different market structures, and builds food inflation measures covering food at home and away from home.
